### B1-01 | The Bicycle City
In the 1970s, the Dutch city of Utrecht was full of cars, and the roads in the centre were dangerous for children. After a number of serious accidents, parents began to protest. They asked the city to make streets safer for walking and cycling. Over the next forty years, the city changed slowly. It built wide bicycle lanes that were separated from traffic, and it closed some central streets to cars completely. In 2019, Utrecht opened the largest bicycle parking garage in the world, next to the main railway station. It has space for about 12,500 bicycles, and the first day of parking is free. Today, more than half of all trips in the city centre are made by bicycle. Shop owners were worried at first that they would lose customers, but many now say business is better, because cyclists stop more often than drivers. Other cities send planners to Utrecht to learn from its experience, although they admit that changing habits takes a long time.

### B1-02 | A Volunteer Doctor
When Dr Amina Yusuf retired from her hospital job at the age of sixty-two, she did not want to stop working completely. A friend told her about a charity that sends medical volunteers to small islands where there is no permanent doctor. Amina joined the programme and spent three months on an island with fewer than two thousand people. She held a clinic every morning in the school building, because there was no hospital. Most patients had simple problems, such as infections or high blood pressure, but once she had to treat a fisherman with a badly injured hand during a storm, when no boat could take him to the mainland. She also trained two local women to check blood pressure and keep medical records, so that some care could continue after she left. Amina says the work was harder than she expected, but it gave her a new purpose. She is now planning her second trip and encouraging other retired doctors to join.

### B1-03 | The Honey Hunters
In some forests of East Africa, people and a small bird work together to find honey. The bird is called the honeyguide. It cannot open bees' nests by itself, but it loves to eat the wax inside them. When it sees a person in the forest, it flies close, makes a special call and then flies from tree to tree towards a nest. The honey hunters follow it, and when they find the nest, they use smoke to calm the bees and cut it open. They take the honey, and the bird eats the wax that is left. Scientists studying the Yao people in Mozambique found that hunters use a particular sound, a loud trill followed by a grunt, to call the birds. When researchers played this sound, birds guided them to nests much more often than when they played other sounds. This is one of very few known examples of a wild animal and humans cooperating in this way, and it has probably existed for thousands of years.

### B1-04 | The Four-Day Week
In 2022, around seventy companies in the United Kingdom took part in a six-month experiment. Their employees worked four days a week instead of five, but they received the same salary. The companies were very different, from banks and software firms to a fish and chip shop. Before the trial began, managers had to find ways to reduce time wasted at work, for example by having shorter meetings and fewer emails. At the end of the experiment, most companies said that productivity had stayed the same or improved. Workers reported less stress and better sleep, and fewer people took sick days. Some employees used their extra day to look after children or older relatives, while others started hobbies. However, the trial was not a success for everyone. A few businesses found it difficult to serve customers who expected them to be open five days, and some staff felt they had to work harder on the remaining days. Most companies decided to continue with the new system.

### B1-05 | The Message in a Bottle
In 2018, a woman walking on a beach in Western Australia noticed an old bottle lying in the sand. Inside it she found a rolled piece of paper with a printed message in German. The message asked the finder to write down where and when they found the bottle and to send it to the German Naval Observatory. Experts later confirmed that the bottle had been thrown into the Indian Ocean from a German ship in June 1886. At that time, scientists used thousands of bottles to study the direction of ocean currents, because there were no satellites. Most of the bottles were never found. This one had probably reached the Australian coast within a year and then stayed buried in the sand for more than a century, which protected the paper. The family who found it lent it to a museum in Perth, where visitors can now see what is believed to be the oldest message in a bottle ever found.

### B1-06 | Learning a Language After Fifty
Many adults believe that it is too late to learn a new language after the age of fifty. Research suggests that this belief is only partly true. Older learners usually find it harder to copy a perfect accent, and they may need more time to remember new words. However, they also have important advantages. They often have better study habits than younger people, a wider knowledge of the world and a large vocabulary in their first language, which helps them guess meanings. A study in Scotland found that adults who learned a second language later in life performed better in tests of attention and memory than people who did not. Teachers recommend that older learners practise for short periods every day instead of studying for hours once a week. Speaking with real people, for example in a conversation club, seems especially useful, because it keeps the brain active and gives learners a reason to continue. The most important thing, experts say, is to enjoy the process.

### B1-07 | The Night Train Returns
For many years, night trains in Europe seemed to be dying out. Cheap flights were faster and often less expensive, so railway companies closed many overnight routes. Recently, however, sleeper trains have become popular again. One reason is climate change: a journey by train produces much less carbon dioxide than the same journey by plane. Another reason is comfort. Passengers can go to sleep in one capital city and wake up in another, without waiting at airports or paying for a hotel. In 2021, the Austrian railway company started a new route between Vienna and Paris, and other companies have followed. The trains are not perfect. Tickets for a private sleeping cabin can be expensive, the trains are sometimes late, and there are not enough modern carriages, because few factories build them. Still, many travellers say the experience feels like an adventure. Governments in several countries now give money to support night trains, hoping that more people will choose them instead of short flights.

### B1-08 | The Girl Who Counted Birds
When Maya was nine, her grandfather gave her a pair of old binoculars and a notebook. Every morning before school, she sat by the kitchen window and wrote down the birds she saw in the garden. After a year, she had recorded twenty-three different species. Her teacher suggested that she send her notes to a national bird-watching project that collects information from thousands of volunteers. Scientists use these records to understand how bird populations are changing. Maya's notebook showed something interesting: a small brown bird called the house sparrow, which used to be common, appeared much less often in the second year. Other volunteers in her region had noticed the same decline. Maya started a campaign at school to plant bushes and put up nesting boxes, because sparrows need places to hide and build nests. Two years later, she counted sparrows in her garden almost every day again. She now wants to study biology at university and become a scientist who protects wild birds.

### B1-09 | Why We Yawn
Everybody yawns, from babies before they are born to old people, and many animals yawn too. Yet scientists still do not completely agree about why. For a long time, people believed that we yawn because our bodies need more oxygen. However, experiments in which people breathed air with extra oxygen did not make them yawn less, so this idea is now considered unlikely. A more popular theory today is that yawning helps to cool the brain. When we yawn, we take in a large amount of air, and the stretching of the jaw increases blood flow to the head. In one study, people yawned less when they held a cold pack against their forehead. Yawning is also contagious: seeing, hearing or even reading about someone yawning can make us do it. This happens more often between friends and family members than between strangers, which suggests it may be connected to empathy. Even dogs sometimes yawn when their owners do. Did you yawn while reading this?

### B1-10 | The Repair Café
In 2009, a journalist in Amsterdam named Martine Postma organised a small event where people could bring broken things and repair them with the help of volunteers. She was tired of seeing useful objects thrown away because nobody knew how to fix them. The idea became the first Repair Café. Today there are more than two thousand Repair Cafés around the world. They usually take place once a month in a community centre or library. Visitors bring toasters, lamps, clothes, bicycles and toys, and volunteers with practical skills show them how to find and solve the problem. The service is free, although many people leave a small donation. Organisers say that about seventy percent of items are successfully repaired. The cafés are not only about saving money and reducing waste. They also bring neighbours together, and many visitors say they enjoy learning a new skill and having a cup of coffee while they wait. Some manufacturers, however, still make products that are almost impossible to open.

### B1-11 | The Silent Disco
At first, a silent disco looks very strange. A room is full of people dancing, singing and laughing, but there is no music in the air. Each dancer is wearing wireless headphones. Usually there are two or three DJs playing different songs at the same time, and the headphones have lights that change colour depending on which channel you are listening to. Silent discos began in the 1990s as a way for festivals to continue late at night without disturbing people living nearby. Since then they have appeared in museums, libraries, mountain tops and even underwater swimming pools. Organisers say they are good for people who are shy, because you can take off the headphones and have a normal conversation at any time. Some schools use them for parties, and hospitals have tried them for patients with dementia, who often respond strongly to music from their youth. Critics say that dancing alone with headphones is less social, but most participants seem to disagree.

### B1-12 | The Forgotten Painter
For more than a century, almost nobody knew the name of the Swedish painter Hilma af Klint. She was born in 1862 and trained at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts in Stockholm, where she earned a living painting landscapes and portraits. Privately, however, she created hundreds of large, colourful abstract paintings full of circles, spirals and symbols. She believed that the world was not ready for these works. Before she died in 1944, she asked that they should stay hidden for at least twenty years after her death. For a long time, art historians said that abstract painting was invented by male artists such as Kandinsky, around 1911. But af Klint had painted abstract works several years earlier, in 1906. When a major exhibition of her paintings opened at the Guggenheim Museum in New York in 2018, it became the most visited show in the museum's history. Her story has made many people ask how many other women artists have been forgotten.

### B1-13 | Living Without Plastic for a Month
Last spring, a family of four in Wales decided to try living for one month without buying any new plastic. They thought it would be quite easy, but the first trip to the supermarket showed them how difficult it was. Almost everything, from pasta to cheese, was wrapped in plastic. They started shopping at a local market, where they could put vegetables directly into cloth bags, and they found a shop that sold rice, oats and soap from large containers. They bought milk in glass bottles from a farm. The children had to give up their favourite crisps and sweets, which caused some arguments. By the end of the month, the family's rubbish had fallen by about two thirds, but shopping took much more time and was slightly more expensive. They decided not to continue with all the rules after the month ended, but they kept the habits that worked best, such as the cloth bags, the market shopping and the glass milk bottles.

### B1-14 | The Tallest Trees
The tallest living tree in the world is a coast redwood in California called Hyperion. It is about 116 metres tall, which is higher than a thirty-storey building. It was discovered in 2006 by two naturalists who spent months searching remote valleys. To protect it, the exact location has never been officially published, and visitors who try to reach it can be fined. Redwoods can live for more than two thousand years. They grow so tall partly because of the climate where they live. In summer, thick fog comes in from the Pacific Ocean, and the trees take water directly from it through their leaves. This helps them survive dry months. Their thick bark contains chemicals that protect them from insects and fire. However, nearly all of the original redwood forests were cut down in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and only about five percent remain. Scientists are now studying how a warmer climate with less fog might affect the trees that are left.

### B1-15 | The Kindness Experiment
A university psychologist wanted to know whether small acts of kindness really make people happier. She divided two hundred volunteers into three groups. For six weeks, the first group had to do five kind things every week, such as helping a neighbour or buying a coffee for a stranger. The second group had to do five new things each week, like trying a different route to work. The third group simply wrote down what they did each day. At the beginning and end of the study, everyone answered questions about their happiness. The results showed that people in the kindness group became clearly happier than people in the other two groups. Interestingly, it did not matter much whether the kind acts were big or small. What mattered most was variety: people who did the same kind act every week felt less benefit than those who changed what they did. The researcher suggests that we notice and enjoy new experiences more than routines.
