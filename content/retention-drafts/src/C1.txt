### C1-01 | The Placebo Paradox
For decades, the placebo effect was treated by medical researchers mainly as a nuisance, a source of noise that had to be controlled in clinical trials so that the real effect of a drug could be measured. Patients who received sugar pills often improved, and this improvement was subtracted from the results. More recently, however, scientists have begun to study the placebo effect as a phenomenon in its own right, and their findings are challenging assumptions about the boundary between mind and body. Brain imaging has shown that when patients expect pain relief from a placebo, their brains release endorphins, the body's natural painkillers, and activity decreases in regions associated with pain. The effect is not limited to pills. Fake injections tend to produce stronger responses than fake tablets, and in some studies, sham surgery, in which patients were given only an incision, produced improvements comparable to real operations for certain knee conditions. Even more surprising are experiments involving so-called open-label placebos. In a study at Harvard, patients with irritable bowel syndrome were told explicitly that they were receiving inert pills containing no medication, yet many of them reported significant relief compared with a group that received no treatment at all. Researchers suggest that the ritual of treatment, together with a caring relationship with a clinician, may trigger the body's own healing mechanisms. The opposite phenomenon, the nocebo effect, is equally powerful: patients warned about possible side effects are more likely to experience them. These findings raise ethical dilemmas. Doctors are generally expected not to deceive patients, but if honest placebos work, they could offer cheap, safe treatment for some conditions. Critics caution that placebos rarely affect the underlying disease, such as a tumour or an infection, and should never replace effective treatment.

### C1-02 | The Tyranny of Choice
Conventional economic thinking holds that more choice is always better: if consumers have more options, they are more likely to find something that matches their preferences precisely, and competition between producers should improve quality and lower prices. In the early 2000s, however, a series of psychological studies questioned this assumption. In the most widely cited experiment, researchers set up a tasting stand in an upmarket supermarket in California, offering either six or twenty-four varieties of jam. The larger display attracted more passers-by, but only about three percent of those who stopped bought a jar, compared with roughly thirty percent of those who saw the smaller selection. The psychologist Barry Schwartz popularised the idea that an abundance of options can lead to paralysis, as people postpone decisions, and to dissatisfaction, as they wonder whether an alternative would have been better. He distinguished between maximisers, who seek the best possible option, and satisficers, who settle for one that is good enough, and suggested that the latter tend to be happier. In recent years, though, the picture has become more complicated. Attempts to replicate the jam study produced mixed results, and a large analysis of dozens of similar experiments concluded that the average effect of choice overload was close to zero. The researchers found that it appeared only under particular conditions: when options were difficult to compare, when people lacked clear preferences, or when they were under time pressure. Businesses have nonetheless drawn practical lessons, such as presenting a recommended default option or grouping products into clear categories. The debate illustrates a broader issue in psychology, where some striking findings have proved harder to reproduce than early publicity suggested. For consumers, the most useful lesson may simply be to decide in advance what matters most before facing a wall of alternatives.

### C1-03 | The Shipping Container Revolution
Few inventions have shaped the modern world as profoundly as the humble steel shipping container, yet it rarely receives the attention given to computers or aircraft. Before the 1950s, loading a cargo ship was a slow, labour-intensive process. Goods arrived at ports in sacks, barrels and crates of every size, and teams of dockworkers carried them on board piece by piece. A ship could spend more time in port than at sea, and theft was common. In 1956, an American trucking entrepreneur named Malcolm McLean loaded fifty-eight metal boxes onto a converted oil tanker in New Jersey and sent them to Texas. His insight was that the box, not the ship, should be the basic unit of transport, moving seamlessly between trucks, trains and vessels without its contents being handled. The economic effects were dramatic. McLean calculated that loading costs fell from almost six dollars per ton to around sixteen cents. However, the transition was neither quick nor smooth. Ports had to invest in enormous cranes and storage areas, and the industry spent years arguing over standard sizes before agreeing on dimensions based on twenty- and forty-foot units. Dockworkers' unions fought the change, with good reason: employment in traditional ports collapsed, and cities such as London and New York lost much of their shipping activity to new, purpose-built container terminals. Economists argue that containerisation did more to expand international trade than many trade agreements, making it economical to manufacture components in one country and assemble them in another. The global supply chains that allow a smartphone to contain parts from dozens of nations depend on it. The disruption caused by the pandemic, when containers ended up in the wrong places, revealed how fragile this efficient system can be.

### C1-04 | Reading the Ocean Floor
It is often said that we have better maps of the surface of Mars than of the floor of our own oceans, and until recently this was close to the truth. Only about a quarter of the seabed has been mapped in high resolution, even though it covers more than seventy percent of the planet. The reason is largely technical. Satellites can measure the shape of the land directly, but water absorbs the radar and light they use, so the ocean floor must be surveyed by ships equipped with sonar, which send sound pulses downwards and measure the echoes. A single vessel covers a narrow strip, and mapping the whole ocean this way would take centuries. In 2017, an international project called Seabed 2030 was launched with the aim of producing a complete map by the end of that decade. Rather than relying solely on dedicated research ships, it collects data from fishing boats, cargo vessels, yachts and autonomous underwater drones, and it encourages governments and companies to share surveys they already possess. Progress has been substantial, although the most remote areas, especially in the Southern Ocean, remain difficult to reach. Detailed seabed maps have many uses. They help predict how tsunamis will travel, guide the placement of undersea internet cables, improve models of ocean currents that influence climate, and reveal habitats such as cold-water coral reefs. They are also essential for navigation: in 2005, an American nuclear submarine crashed into an uncharted underwater mountain near Guam, injuring dozens of sailors. Some scientists, however, worry that better maps will make it easier for companies to exploit the deep sea for minerals before its ecosystems are properly understood. Balancing the scientific value of mapping against the risk of commercial exploitation will be one of the major challenges for ocean governance in coming decades.

### C1-05 | The Language of Smell
English speakers have a rich vocabulary for colours, but when it comes to smells, they struggle. Asked to name a familiar odour such as cinnamon or coffee without seeing its source, most people perform surprisingly poorly, often resorting to vague descriptions or comparisons with objects, such as smells like a hospital. For a long time, this difficulty was assumed to be a universal feature of human biology: our sense of smell, it was argued, was simply less connected to language than vision. Research by the linguist Asifa Majid and her colleagues has challenged this assumption. Working with the Jahai, a group of hunter-gatherers living in the rainforests of Malaysia, they found that speakers used a set of abstract words that refer to smells in the same way that red or blue refers to colours, independently of any specific source. One term, for example, describes the scent shared by petrol, bat droppings and certain mushrooms. In experiments, Jahai participants named smells as quickly and consistently as they named colours, while a comparison group of English speakers took far longer and agreed far less. Further studies with other groups in South-East Asia and Mexico found similar abilities. Interestingly, related farming communities nearby did not share this vocabulary, which suggests that culture and lifestyle, rather than genes, play a decisive role. The researchers propose that the Western neglect of smell may be partly cultural, reinforced by philosophical traditions that ranked it as the lowest of the senses. Training can make a difference: wine experts and perfumers, who practise describing aromas, develop a more precise vocabulary. The findings have implications for medicine as well, since loss of smell is an early sign of several neurological diseases.

### C1-06 | Cities That Sink
Jakarta, the capital of Indonesia and home to more than ten million people, is sinking faster than almost any other large city in the world. In some northern districts the ground has dropped by more than four metres over recent decades, and parts of the coastline now lie below sea level, protected by sea walls that are themselves subsiding. While rising sea levels caused by climate change contribute to the danger, the main cause of the sinking is local. Much of the city lacks a reliable piped water supply, so residents and businesses pump water from underground aquifers. As the water is removed, the layers of clay and sand above compress, and the land settles. The enormous weight of buildings adds to the pressure. Jakarta is not unique. Mexico City, built on the bed of a former lake, has sunk by around ten metres over the past century, damaging historic churches and the underground railway. Parts of Shanghai, Bangkok and Houston have experienced similar problems. The solutions are well understood but difficult to implement. Tokyo provides an encouraging example: after severe subsidence in the twentieth century, the city strictly limited groundwater extraction and built a large surface water supply network, and the sinking almost stopped. In Jakarta, the government has begun to expand piped water services and has proposed a giant sea wall across the bay. In 2019, the president also announced that the capital would move to a new city on the island of Borneo, though critics argued that this would do little for the millions who would remain behind and could damage rainforest. Experts emphasise that even if pumping stops, land that has already sunk will not rise again.

### C1-07 | The Ethics of De-extinction
In 2003, scientists in Spain briefly achieved something that had never been done before: they brought an extinct animal back to life. The bucardo, a wild mountain goat from the Pyrenees, had died out three years earlier when the last female was killed by a falling tree. Researchers had preserved some of her cells, and using cloning techniques, they implanted embryos into domestic goats. One kid was born alive, but it died within minutes because of a lung defect. Since then, advances in genetic engineering have made the idea of reviving lost species seem less like science fiction. A company based in the United States has announced plans to create animals resembling the woolly mammoth by editing the genes of Asian elephants, with the aim of releasing them into the Arctic tundra. Supporters argue that such projects could restore ecosystems; mammoths, they claim, would trample snow and knock down trees, helping to keep the permafrost frozen and preventing the release of greenhouse gases. They also believe the technology could help endangered species by restoring lost genetic diversity. Critics raise several objections. Recreated animals would not truly be the original species but genetically modified hybrids, and they might suffer if raised without parents of their own kind, especially social animals that learn behaviour from their group. The habitats in which extinct species lived may no longer exist. There is also a risk that the promise of resurrection could weaken efforts to protect species that are still alive, if people come to believe that extinction is reversible. Some ecologists argue that the money involved would achieve far more if it were spent on conserving existing habitats. For now, the debate concerns possibilities rather than reality, but it forces us to consider what responsibilities humans have towards nature.

### C1-08 | The Myth of Multitasking
Many people pride themselves on their ability to multitask, answering emails during meetings, listening to podcasts while studying or checking messages while driving. Research in cognitive psychology suggests that, for most tasks requiring attention, what we call multitasking is actually rapid switching between activities, and each switch carries a cost. When the brain moves from one task to another, it must disengage from the first set of rules and activate the second, a process that can take a fraction of a second but accumulates over time. Studies have found that people who alternate between tasks make more errors and take significantly longer to complete them than people who do the same tasks one after the other. A widely cited study at Stanford University compared students who frequently used several media at once with those who rarely did. Surprisingly, the heavy multitaskers performed worse on tests of filtering out irrelevant information and switching between tasks, the very abilities they might have been expected to develop. The researchers could not determine whether multitasking caused these weaknesses or whether people who were already easily distracted were drawn to it. There are exceptions. Simple, highly practised activities, such as walking, can usually be combined with conversation, because one of them demands little conscious control. A very small proportion of people, sometimes called supertaskers, appear able to perform two demanding tasks at once with little loss. In one driving simulator experiment, only about two and a half percent of participants managed it. The practical implications are clear: setting aside periods for focused work, turning off notifications and batching similar tasks together are likely to improve both productivity and accuracy. For drivers, the risks of divided attention can be fatal.

### C1-09 | The Great Stink
In the hot summer of 1858, the River Thames in London smelled so terrible that the period became known as the Great Stink. For centuries, the river had served as the city's main sewer, receiving human waste, industrial chemicals and the remains of slaughtered animals. The problem had grown worse as London's population exploded and as the introduction of flushing toilets sent more waste directly into the river through old drains. Many people believed that diseases such as cholera were caused by foul air, so the stench caused genuine panic. Members of Parliament, whose building stood beside the river, soaked the curtains in chemicals in an attempt to block the smell, and some proposed moving their sessions elsewhere. The crisis finally pushed politicians to act. Within weeks, they approved funding for an ambitious plan by the engineer Joseph Bazalgette. His team built more than one hundred and thirty kilometres of large brick sewers, which intercepted waste before it reached the central Thames and carried it eastwards, downstream of the city. Bazalgette made a decision that proved remarkably far-sighted: he made the pipes roughly twice as large as the population of the time required, reasoning that the work should only be done once. Parts of the embankments built to hide the sewers along the river became roads and gardens that Londoners still use today. Ironically, the theory that justified the project was wrong; cholera spreads through contaminated water, not bad air. Nevertheless, by separating sewage from drinking water sources, the system ended major cholera outbreaks in the capital. More than a century and a half later, the old sewers are overflowing during heavy rain, and a new super sewer tunnel beneath the river was completed in the 2020s.

### C1-10 | Universal Basic Income Experiments
The idea of a universal basic income, a regular payment given to every citizen regardless of whether they work, has moved from the margins of political debate towards the mainstream. Supporters on the left see it as a way to reduce poverty and give workers more bargaining power, while some on the right favour it as a simpler alternative to complex welfare bureaucracies. Interest has grown with fears that automation and artificial intelligence could eliminate many jobs. Critics, however, warn that it would be enormously expensive and might discourage people from working. Because a national scheme has never been implemented, researchers have turned to experiments. Between 2017 and 2018, Finland gave two thousand unemployed people a monthly payment of five hundred and sixty euros, which they continued to receive even if they found work. The participants reported better mental health, less stress and greater trust in institutions than a control group, and their employment rose slightly, contradicting the fear that free money would make people idle. In Kenya, a charity called GiveDirectly has been running a long-term study in which entire villages receive regular cash transfers for up to twelve years. Early findings indicate that many recipients invested in businesses or livestock rather than spending the money on alcohol or tobacco, as some had predicted. Economists caution against drawing firm conclusions. Most trials have been small, temporary and targeted at specific groups, so they cannot reveal how a permanent universal payment would affect taxes, prices or the wider labour market. People may also behave differently when they know a scheme will end. A truly universal scheme would also need to be funded, which would probably require substantially higher taxes, and that political challenge remains unresolved.

### C1-11 | The Science of Laughter
Laughter is often regarded as a simple response to humour, but research suggests that it is primarily a social signal rather than a reaction to jokes. The psychologist Robert Provine spent years recording laughter in natural settings, such as shopping centres and university campuses. He found that most laughter followed ordinary remarks that were not particularly funny, such as greetings or comments about the weather. Speakers laughed more than listeners, and people were about thirty times more likely to laugh when they were with others than when they were alone. Provine concluded that laughter evolved to strengthen social bonds and to signal friendly intentions. Its roots appear to be ancient. Chimpanzees, gorillas and orangutans produce panting vocalisations during play and tickling, which researchers believe are related to human laughter. Even rats emit high-pitched chirps, inaudible to humans, when they are tickled, and they seek out the hand that tickles them. Neuroscientists distinguish between spontaneous laughter, which is difficult to fake convincingly, and deliberate, polite laughter. Listeners can usually tell the difference, and studies suggest that they judge relationships partly on this basis: spontaneous laughter between people is interpreted as a sign of friendship. Laughter also has physiological effects. It appears to trigger the release of endorphins, and in one experiment, participants who had watched a comedy together tolerated pain for longer than those who had watched a documentary. Some therapists use laughter yoga, in which groups begin by laughing artificially until it becomes genuine, although evidence for its health benefits remains limited. Contagion plays a role too, which is why television comedies have traditionally added recorded laughter, a technique that makes viewers laugh more even though most claim to dislike it.

### C1-12 | Bilingual Brains
For much of the twentieth century, parents in many countries were advised not to raise their children with two languages. Educators feared that bilingualism would confuse children, delay their speech and harm their academic performance. Early studies seemed to support this view, but they were often flawed, comparing immigrant children from poorer families with wealthier monolingual peers and testing them in their weaker language. A turning point came in 1962, when a study in Montreal that carefully controlled for social background found that bilingual children actually outperformed monolinguals on a range of tests. Since then, a large body of research has explored whether managing two languages provides cognitive benefits beyond language itself. Because both languages remain active in the brain even when only one is being used, bilinguals must constantly suppress the irrelevant one. Some researchers argued that this practice strengthens executive functions, the mental skills used to control attention, switch between tasks and ignore distractions. Particularly striking were studies suggesting that lifelong bilinguals developed symptoms of dementia four to five years later, on average, than monolinguals with similar brain damage. However, the so-called bilingual advantage has become controversial. Several large studies have failed to find differences in executive function between bilingual and monolingual adults, and some scientists suspect that positive results were more likely to be published than negative ones. The evidence regarding dementia is also mixed. What is not disputed is that bilingualism causes no harm to cognitive development. Children who learn two languages may initially have smaller vocabularies in each, but their combined vocabulary is typically similar to that of monolingual children, and they catch up. The practical, cultural and social value of speaking more than one language is, of course, considerable in itself.

### C1-13 | The Underground Network of Forests
Walk through a forest and you may see individual trees competing for light, but beneath your feet lies a hidden network that connects many of them. The roots of most plants form partnerships with fungi, whose thread-like filaments, called hyphae, spread through the soil. The fungi help trees absorb water and nutrients such as phosphorus, and in exchange they receive sugars produced by photosynthesis. In the 1990s, the Canadian ecologist Suzanne Simard published research suggesting that these fungal networks also link trees to one another, allowing carbon to move between them. Using radioactive isotopes, she traced carbon passing from paper birch trees to Douglas firs, particularly when the firs were shaded. Later work led to the popular idea of a wood wide web, in which large mother trees support their seedlings and trees warn neighbours of insect attacks through chemical signals. The concept captured public imagination and featured in bestselling books and films. In recent years, however, several scientists have argued that the popular story has gone far beyond the evidence. A review published in 2023 examined the field studies and concluded that claims about mother trees deliberately nurturing their offspring, or about networks being widespread in forests, were not well supported. Carbon may move through soil by other routes, and it is often unclear whether transfers benefit the receiving tree in a meaningful way. Fungi, the critics point out, have their own interests and are not simply pipelines serving trees. Simard and her supporters maintain that the core findings are sound. The debate shows how a scientific idea can be simplified as it spreads to the public, and it is a reminder that much about forest soils remains unknown.

### C1-14 | The Cost of Free Apps
Most of the applications on our phones cost nothing to download, yet the companies that make them are among the most valuable in the world. The explanation lies in a business model based on data. Many free apps collect information about users, including their location, contacts, browsing habits and the time they spend on different screens, and use it to sell targeted advertising. In 2018, it emerged that the political consultancy Cambridge Analytica had obtained data from millions of Facebook users through a personality quiz app. Only a few hundred thousand people had installed the app, but it also gathered information about their friends. The scandal led to investigations in several countries and a significant fine. Researchers who have analysed the code of popular apps have found that many share data with dozens of third-party companies, often without users being clearly informed. A study of children's apps found that a majority appeared to collect information in ways that might break laws designed to protect young users. Regulators have responded. The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation, which came into force in 2018, requires companies to explain clearly what data they collect and to obtain consent, and it allows fines of up to four percent of global annual turnover. Apple introduced a feature in 2021 that asks users whether they want to allow apps to track them across other companies' apps and websites; most chose not to, which cost some advertising businesses billions of dollars. Critics argue, nonetheless, that consent forms are usually too long and complex for anyone to read, so people agree without understanding. Some propose that companies should be prohibited from collecting certain kinds of data at all, rather than simply asking permission.

### C1-15 | The Return of the Wolves to Yellowstone
When grey wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in the United States in 1995, after an absence of about seventy years, the event was celebrated as a landmark in conservation. Over the following decades, a remarkable story spread through documentaries and textbooks. According to this account, the wolves reduced the number of elk, which had been grazing heavily on young willow and aspen trees along rivers. As the trees recovered, riverbanks stabilised, beavers returned, and birds and fish flourished. Some versions went further, claiming that the wolves had literally changed the course of rivers. The idea illustrated an ecological concept known as a trophic cascade, in which a top predator influences an entire food chain. Part of the explanation was thought to be behavioural: elk, afraid of being hunted, avoided open valleys where they were vulnerable, a phenomenon described as the ecology of fear. Recent research suggests the reality is more complicated. Elk numbers did fall, but other factors contributed significantly, including hunting by humans outside the park, severe winters, droughts and the growing population of bears and cougars. Some studies found that elk did not avoid risky areas as much as expected, and that willow recovery has been patchy rather than universal. In places where beavers had disappeared, streams had cut deeper channels, lowering the water table, so young trees struggled even when grazing decreased. Many ecologists now argue that removing a predator can alter an ecosystem in ways that are not easily reversed simply by bringing it back. None of this means that the reintroduction failed. The wolves have thrived, attracting tourists who bring millions of dollars to local communities. But the story shows the danger of turning complex science into a simple fable.
