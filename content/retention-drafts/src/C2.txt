### C2-01 | The Measurement Problem in Happiness Economics
For most of the twentieth century, economists measured national progress almost exclusively through gross domestic product, the total value of goods and services an economy produces. Its architect, Simon Kuznets, warned the United States Congress in 1934 that the welfare of a nation could scarcely be inferred from a measure of national income, yet his caution was largely forgotten as GDP growth became the dominant objective of governments worldwide. In 1974, the economist Richard Easterlin published a paper that unsettled this consensus. Drawing on surveys in which people rated their own happiness, he observed that within a given country, richer individuals reported greater happiness than poorer ones, but that as countries became wealthier over time, average happiness did not appear to rise accordingly. This apparent contradiction, which became known as the Easterlin paradox, prompted a flourishing field of research into subjective well-being. Explanations proliferated. One holds that people judge their situation relative to others, so that a general rise in income leaves relative positions unchanged. Another invokes hedonic adaptation, the tendency to become accustomed to improvements, so that a larger house or a higher salary quickly comes to feel normal. The paradox has not gone unchallenged. Using larger international datasets, economists Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers argued in 2008 that life satisfaction does increase with national income, roughly in proportion to its logarithm, so that additional wealth matters less as countries grow richer but never ceases to matter entirely. Part of the disagreement turns on methodology. Questions about momentary happiness and questions asking people to evaluate their life as a whole capture different things, and responses can be swayed by trivial factors such as the weather on the day of the survey or the order of preceding questions. Moreover, the numerical scales respondents use may not be comparable across cultures or generations. Despite these difficulties, several governments have begun incorporating well-being indicators into policy. Bhutan famously promotes gross national happiness, and in 2019 New Zealand introduced a well-being budget that directed spending towards mental health and child poverty. Sceptics contend that such initiatives risk becoming exercises in branding unless the underlying measures are robust enough to guide difficult trade-offs. Others respond that imperfect measures of well-being are still preferable to ignoring well-being altogether. Whatever the outcome of the debate, the question of what societies should ultimately be trying to maximise has returned to the centre of economic thinking, after decades in which the answer seemed obvious.

### C2-02 | Translating the Untranslatable
Translators have long been haunted by the Italian maxim traduttore, traditore, which equates the translator with a traitor. The pun itself illustrates the problem: rendered into English, the near-identity of the two words, and with it the joke, evaporates. Every language carves up experience in its own way, and the translator must constantly decide which aspects of an original to preserve at the expense of others. Should a poem retain its rhyme scheme if doing so forces the translator to distort its meaning? Should a culturally specific reference be explained, replaced with a local equivalent, or left unfamiliar so that readers sense the foreignness of the text? The German theologian Friedrich Schleiermacher framed this dilemma in 1813 in terms that are still cited: the translator can either leave the author in peace and move the reader towards him, or leave the reader in peace and move the author towards the reader. Two centuries later, the American scholar Lawrence Venuti revived the distinction, criticising what he called the translator's invisibility. In the English-speaking publishing world, he argued, translations are praised when they read fluently, as if originally written in English, a practice that erases cultural difference and renders the translator's labour unseen. He advocated instead a foreignising approach that deliberately preserves unusual syntax or vocabulary. Critics responded that such strategies can produce prose that is merely awkward, alienating readers without conveying anything the original audience would have experienced. The debate has acquired new urgency with the arrival of neural machine translation, which produces remarkably fluent output by learning statistical patterns from vast collections of existing translations. Such systems excel at routine documents but tend to flatten stylistic idiosyncrasy and may reproduce biases present in their training data, for example by assigning gender stereotypically when translating from languages whose pronouns do not specify it. Literary translators note that ambiguity is often intentional in fiction and poetry, and that resolving it automatically can destroy meaning. Perhaps the most persuasive defence of translation came from the novelist Umberto Eco, who described it as the art of negotiation: an attempt not to say exactly the same thing in another language, which is impossible, but to say almost the same thing, with each choice made consciously. Many contemporary translators embrace this view, describing their work as a form of rewriting that demands creativity as much as linguistic competence. Some publishers have responded by printing translators' names on book covers, a small but symbolic recognition of their contribution.

### C2-03 | The Antibiotic Cliff
When Alexander Fleming accepted the Nobel Prize in 1945 for the discovery of penicillin, he used part of his lecture to issue a warning. It was not difficult, he observed, to make microbes resistant to penicillin in the laboratory by exposing them to concentrations insufficient to kill them, and the same thing could happen in the human body if people took too little of the drug or stopped too soon. His prediction proved prescient. Resistance arises through natural selection: in any large population of bacteria, a few individuals may carry mutations allowing them to survive an antibiotic, and when susceptible competitors are eliminated, these survivors multiply. Bacteria can also exchange genetic material directly, passing resistance genes between unrelated species on small loops of DNA called plasmids. Overuse accelerates the process. Antibiotics are frequently prescribed for viral infections such as colds, against which they are useless, and in many countries they are sold without prescription. In agriculture, they have been routinely added to animal feed, not to treat disease but to promote faster growth, a practice the European Union banned in 2006 but which persists elsewhere. A landmark analysis published in 2022 estimated that bacterial antimicrobial resistance was directly responsible for around 1.27 million deaths in 2019, more than HIV or malaria. Paradoxically, while the need for new drugs has grown, the pipeline has dwindled. Few new classes of antibiotics have reached the market since the 1980s, partly because the economics are unfavourable. Unlike medicines for chronic conditions, antibiotics are taken for a short course, and new ones are deliberately held in reserve to delay resistance, so sales remain low even when a drug is valuable. Several promising start-ups have gone bankrupt shortly after winning regulatory approval. Governments are experimenting with alternative models. The United Kingdom pioneered a subscription arrangement in which the health service pays companies a fixed annual fee for access to certain antibiotics, regardless of the quantity used, effectively treating them like a fire service that is funded to be available rather than paid per emergency. Researchers are also exploring alternatives, including viruses called bacteriophages that infect specific bacteria, and artificial intelligence has already identified novel antibacterial compounds by screening millions of molecules. None of these approaches offers a quick solution, and experts stress that preserving existing drugs through careful prescribing remains essential. The challenge, they argue, is as much economic and behavioural as it is scientific.

### C2-04 | The Afterlife of Dead Languages
Linguists estimate that of the roughly seven thousand languages spoken today, around half may cease to be spoken by the end of this century. Most are not destroyed by conquest in any dramatic sense but abandoned gradually, as parents in minority communities, conscious of the economic advantages of a dominant language, stop transmitting their own to their children. Once a language is no longer acquired by infants, its decline is usually irreversible within a few generations. Yet the history of Hebrew suggests that the boundary between living and dead languages is not absolute. For nearly two millennia Hebrew survived primarily as a liturgical and scholarly language, read and written but rarely used for everyday conversation. In the late nineteenth century, Eliezer Ben-Yehuda, who had settled in Jerusalem, resolved that his household would speak only Hebrew, and his son is often described as the first native speaker in modern times. Ben-Yehuda coined or revived thousands of words for contemporary concepts and compiled a monumental dictionary. His personal determination alone would hardly have sufficed, however; the revival succeeded because schools adopted Hebrew as the language of instruction and because a growing community had practical reasons to share a common tongue. Modern Israeli Hebrew differs in significant respects from its classical predecessor, and some linguists argue that its grammar and sounds owe much to the European languages its early speakers brought with them. Elsewhere, revitalisation efforts have drawn on different resources. Speakers of Welsh have benefited from bilingual education and broadcasting, and the number of young speakers has stabilised after a long decline. In New Zealand, so-called language nests, preschools in which elders speak exclusively in Māori to young children, have inspired similar initiatives from Hawaii to Scandinavia. Perhaps most remarkable is the case of Wampanoag, a language of southern New England that had no fluent speakers for over a century. Beginning in the 1990s, the linguist Jessie Little Doe Baird, herself a member of the community, used colonial-era documents, including a seventeenth-century translation of the Bible, to reconstruct its grammar and vocabulary. Her daughter became the first native speaker in generations. Such successes remain exceptional, and they depend on sustained commitment, but they challenge the fatalism with which language death is often regarded. Linguists increasingly work in partnership with communities rather than merely documenting languages for archives. Recordings, dictionaries and digital tools can support revival, but only speakers themselves can decide whether a language will be passed on.

### C2-05 | The Invisible Hand of Default Settings
In the early 2000s, a team of economists examined why organ donation rates in some European countries exceeded ninety percent while neighbouring countries with similar cultures struggled to reach fifteen percent. The difference, they found, had little to do with religious beliefs, public awareness campaigns or attitudes towards medicine. It lay almost entirely in the design of the registration form. In countries where citizens were presumed to consent unless they actively opted out, nearly everyone remained a donor; in countries requiring people to opt in, most never did. This striking illustration of the power of defaults became a cornerstone of behavioural economics, a discipline that combines insights from psychology with economic analysis to explain systematic departures from purely rational decision-making. Defaults exert influence for several reasons. Changing them requires effort, however minimal; they may be interpreted as an implicit recommendation from authorities; and people tend to weigh potential losses from abandoning the status quo more heavily than equivalent gains. In 2008, the economist Richard Thaler and the legal scholar Cass Sunstein popularised the term nudge to describe interventions that steer choices without restricting options or significantly altering financial incentives. Governments enthusiastically embraced the approach. The British government established a Behavioural Insights Team, informally known as the Nudge Unit, which reported, for example, that telling late taxpayers that most people in their area had already paid increased payment rates. Automatic enrolment in workplace pension schemes, with the freedom to leave, dramatically increased retirement savings in Britain and the United States. Nonetheless, the movement has attracted criticism from several directions. Some philosophers object that nudges manipulate people by exploiting cognitive biases rather than engaging their reasoning, and that their influence is ethically problematic precisely because it often goes unnoticed. Others question their effectiveness. A large analysis published in 2022 initially suggested substantial average effects, but subsequent re-analyses argued that, once the tendency of journals to publish positive results was taken into account, the evidence for many types of nudge was weak. Even the organ donation example has proved more nuanced than early accounts implied, since in practice doctors in opt-out countries usually consult families before removing organs, and actual donation rates depend heavily on hospital infrastructure. Advocates now tend to present nudges as modest complements to traditional policy tools rather than substitutes for them. The debate has encouraged more rigorous testing of interventions before they are adopted at scale. It has also raised broader questions about transparency, including whether citizens should always be told when their choices are being deliberately shaped by policymakers.

### C2-06 | The Paradox of the Lighthouse
Economics textbooks have long used the lighthouse as the quintessential example of a public good, a service from which nobody can be excluded and whose use by one person does not diminish its availability to others. A ship that benefits from a lighthouse's beam does not prevent another ship from seeing it, and the keeper has no practical means of charging only those vessels that look. Because individuals can enjoy such goods without paying, the reasoning goes, private markets will supply too few of them, and provision must therefore fall to governments funded by taxation. John Stuart Mill invoked lighthouses in this context in the nineteenth century, and the example was repeated by generations of economists, including Paul Samuelson in his enormously influential textbook. In 1974, however, the economist Ronald Coase published an article with the provocative title The Lighthouse in Economics. Having investigated the actual history of British lighthouses, he reported that many had been built and operated by private individuals, who obtained the right from the Crown to collect dues from ships at nearby ports. The fees were calculated according to the vessel's size and the number of lighthouses it passed, and they were collected by port agents when ships docked. Coase argued that economists had relied on a convenient abstraction without examining the institutions that had actually solved the problem, and he used the case to criticise what he regarded as a habit of theorising detached from empirical reality. His article has itself been subject to revision. Later historians pointed out that the private lighthouse owners depended on legal privileges granted and enforced by the state, without which they could not have compelled payment, and that the system was widely criticised for high charges and inefficiency before being consolidated under a public authority in 1836. Some scholars therefore see the lighthouse as an example not of purely private provision but of a hybrid arrangement in which government created the framework within which private actors operated. The episode remains instructive for contemporary debates about goods such as scientific research, open-source software and broadcast information, where technological change has altered the feasibility of excluding non-payers. It also serves as a reminder that canonical examples, repeated often enough, can acquire an authority their factual basis may not support. Coase himself later received the Nobel Prize, partly for his insistence that economic theory should be grounded in the study of real institutions. His lighthouse essay remains widely assigned to students as a lesson in scepticism.

### C2-07 | Deep Time and the Anthropocene
In 2000, at a scientific conference in Mexico, the atmospheric chemist Paul Crutzen reportedly grew impatient as colleagues repeatedly referred to the Holocene, the geological epoch that began roughly 11,700 years ago at the end of the last ice age. We are no longer in the Holocene, he interjected, but in the Anthropocene. The term, implying that humanity has become a geological force comparable to volcanoes or glaciers, spread rapidly through the sciences and humanities. Geology, however, has formal procedures for defining divisions of time, overseen by the International Commission on Stratigraphy. A new epoch requires a clear, globally synchronous signal preserved in rock or sediment, ideally marked by a so-called golden spike at a specific reference site. In 2009 a working group was established to evaluate the proposal. Its members debated several candidate starting points. Some favoured the beginning of agriculture thousands of years ago, others the arrival of Europeans in the Americas, which caused a catastrophic population collapse and a measurable dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610. The majority eventually preferred the mid-twentieth century, when the so-called Great Acceleration of population, industrial production and resource use left distinctive traces worldwide, including plastics, fly ash from power stations and, most usefully, radioactive plutonium from nuclear weapons tests. In 2023 the group selected Crawford Lake in Canada, whose undisturbed annual layers of sediment record these changes with unusual precision, as the proposed reference site. The following year, though, a committee of the commission voted decisively against recognising the Anthropocene as a formal epoch. Opponents argued that seventy years is a geologically trivial span, that human influence has been diachronic, emerging at different times in different places, and that defining a precise start date obscures this complexity. Some suggested that the Anthropocene would be better understood as an event, like the Great Oxidation Event two and a half billion years ago, an ongoing transformation rather than a neatly bounded interval. Members of the working group expressed frustration, noting that the vote did not dispute the reality or scale of human impact. Regardless of its formal status, the concept continues to shape discussions in fields ranging from ecology to philosophy, where it prompts questions about responsibility, inequality and which humans, precisely, are implicated in the word anthropos. Supporters of the concept insist that whatever geologists decide, the evidence of human influence will remain written in sediments for millions of years. Future observers, they suggest, would have little difficulty recognising the layer.

### C2-08 | The Strange Economics of Superstar Markets
In 1981, the economist Sherwin Rosen published a short paper seeking to explain a puzzling feature of certain professions: why small differences in talent can translate into enormous differences in income. In fields such as classical music, sport, literature and entertainment, a handful of performers earn vast sums while many others who are only marginally less skilled struggle to make a living. Rosen argued that two conditions produce what he called the economics of superstars. First, consumers have a strong preference for the very best, since a lesser substitute is not an adequate replacement; hearing ten mediocre singers does not equal hearing one outstanding performance. Second, technology allows the top performers to serve an enormous audience at little additional cost. A nineteenth-century singer could perform only for those who could fit into a concert hall, but recordings, radio and television enabled a single artist to reach millions simultaneously, displacing local performers. Rosen's insight has acquired new relevance in the digital economy. Streaming platforms make virtually every song ever recorded instantly available, and analysis of their data reveals extreme concentration: a tiny fraction of artists accounts for the overwhelming majority of streams. Similar dynamics appear in technology markets, where network effects reinforce dominance, since a social platform becomes more valuable as more people join it. Some economists contend that superstar effects have spread to corporate leadership and finance, contributing to rising inequality. Executive pay, for instance, has increased far faster than average wages, and one influential explanation holds that as firms grow larger, the value of hiring a marginally more capable chief executive multiplies. Critics counter that this account understates the role of weak governance and boards that are too close to the managers whose pay they set. Interestingly, the internet was once expected to produce the opposite outcome. In 2004, the journalist Chris Anderson popularised the long tail hypothesis, predicting that because online retailers face negligible storage costs, niche products would collectively capture a growing share of sales, eroding the dominance of hits. Subsequent research has found evidence for both trends: the variety of items consumed has expanded, yet the share captured by the most popular items has, in many markets, increased. Recommendation algorithms, which tend to promote what is already popular, may amplify this concentration. Policymakers have debated whether such concentration requires intervention, for example through competition law or fairer royalty arrangements for artists. Rosen himself was cautious, emphasising that superstar markets reflect consumer preferences as much as any failure of the market.

### C2-09 | Volcanoes and the Year Without a Summer
In April 1815, Mount Tambora, on the Indonesian island of Sumbawa, produced the most powerful volcanic eruption in recorded history. The explosions were heard more than two thousand kilometres away, and pyroclastic flows and subsequent famine killed tens of thousands of people in the region. The consequences, however, extended far beyond Indonesia. The eruption injected enormous quantities of sulphur dioxide into the stratosphere, where it formed a veil of sulphate aerosols that spread around the globe and reflected sunlight back into space. Average global temperatures fell by an estimated half a degree to one degree Celsius, enough to disrupt agriculture across the Northern Hemisphere. In 1816, which became known as the year without a summer, snow fell in New England in June, and frosts destroyed crops in July and August. In Europe, still recovering from the Napoleonic Wars, persistent rain and cold caused harvests to fail, grain prices soared, and food riots broke out in Britain, France and Switzerland. Thousands of people emigrated from central Europe and from the north-eastern United States towards the American Midwest in search of more favourable conditions. Contemporaries had no idea that a distant volcano was responsible; the connection was established only in the twentieth century, through the analysis of ice cores and historical records. The cultural reverberations were equally striking. Confined indoors by incessant storms at a villa near Lake Geneva, a group of English writers held a competition to compose ghost stories, from which emerged Mary Shelley's Frankenstein. Some historians have suggested that the vivid orange skies painted by J. M. W. Turner in the following years reflect the aerosols lingering in the atmosphere, and a study of paintings from the period found a correlation between volcanic activity and the redness of depicted sunsets. It has even been claimed, more speculatively, that the high cost of oats for horses encouraged the German inventor Karl Drais to develop an early two-wheeled running machine, a precursor of the bicycle. Today Tambora serves as a sobering model for scientists assessing the vulnerability of a densely populated, interconnected world to a comparable eruption, which could disrupt global food supplies far more severely than in 1816. It also informs debates about solar geoengineering, the controversial proposal to cool the planet deliberately by injecting reflective particles into the stratosphere. Climate scientists warn that such schemes could alter rainfall patterns unpredictably, potentially harming the very regions that are most vulnerable. Tambora's legacy thus remains surprisingly relevant two centuries after the eruption.

### C2-10 | The Fragility of Expertise
In the 1950s, the psychologist Paul Meehl published a slim book that provoked lasting controversy among clinicians. Reviewing some twenty studies, he found that simple statistical formulas, combining a few variables mechanically, predicted outcomes such as academic success, criminal reoffending and psychiatric prognosis at least as accurately as trained professionals using their judgement, and often more so. Subsequent research has confirmed this pattern across a remarkable range of domains, from the selection of employees to the diagnosis of certain medical conditions. Why should experienced experts be outperformed by crude algorithms? One reason is inconsistency. Presented with the same case twice, professionals frequently reach different conclusions, influenced by fatigue, mood or irrelevant context, whereas a formula always returns the same answer. A frequently cited study of judges in Israel reported that favourable parole decisions became steadily less likely as a session wore on and rose sharply after food breaks, although critics have since argued that the scheduling of cases may partly explain the effect. Another reason is that humans are prone to perceiving patterns in noise and to placing excessive weight on vivid but unrepresentative information. Yet expertise is not uniformly unreliable. The psychologist Gary Klein, studying firefighters, nurses and military commanders, documented how experienced professionals often make rapid, accurate decisions by recognising familiar patterns, an ability he termed recognition-primed decision-making. In 2009, Klein and Daniel Kahneman, a leading figure in research on cognitive biases, co-authored a paper attempting to reconcile their apparently opposing views. They concluded that intuitive expertise can be trusted when two conditions are met: the environment must be sufficiently regular to be predictable, and the practitioner must have had extensive opportunity to learn its regularities through rapid, unambiguous feedback. Chess players and anaesthetists generally satisfy these conditions; stock pickers and long-term political forecasters generally do not. This framework helps explain the findings of the political scientist Philip Tetlock, whose twenty-year study of expert predictions found that many specialists performed barely better than chance, with the most confident and media-prominent often faring worst. Tetlock later showed that some amateur forecasters, characterised by open-mindedness and a willingness to update their views incrementally, consistently outperformed professional intelligence analysts. The lesson is not that expertise is worthless, but that confidence is a poor guide to accuracy. Organisations increasingly try to combine the strengths of human judgement and algorithms, for instance by using structured checklists. Such approaches reduce noise without discarding the knowledge that experienced professionals bring.

### C2-11 | Urban Heat and the Politics of Shade
On a hot summer afternoon, the temperature in the centre of a large city can exceed that of the surrounding countryside by several degrees, a phenomenon known as the urban heat island effect. Dark surfaces such as asphalt and roofing absorb solar radiation during the day and release it slowly at night, while buildings trap heat and obstruct cooling breezes, and air conditioners, vehicles and industry emit additional warmth. The scarcity of vegetation compounds the problem, since trees and plants cool their surroundings both by providing shade and by releasing water vapour through their leaves. As climate change makes heatwaves more frequent and intense, the effect has become a serious public health concern. Extreme heat kills more people in many countries than any other weather-related hazard, though its victims, disproportionately elderly people living alone, often die unseen in overheated apartments. The European heatwave of 2003 was associated with tens of thousands of excess deaths, prompting governments to develop early warning systems and to designate cooling centres. Researchers have also documented striking inequalities within cities. In the United States, studies have found that neighbourhoods subjected to the discriminatory lending practice known as redlining in the 1930s, in which predominantly Black and immigrant districts were marked as hazardous for investment, remain on average considerably hotter today than wealthier, historically favoured areas, partly because they have fewer trees and more paved surfaces. Cities are experimenting with an array of responses. Los Angeles has coated streets with a reflective grey sealant that can lower surface temperatures substantially, although some studies suggest reflected radiation may increase the heat experienced by pedestrians. Paris is converting schoolyards into green oases open to the public on weekends, and Medellín in Colombia created a network of green corridors along roads and waterways that reportedly lowered local temperatures by around two degrees. Several cities, including Athens and Miami, have appointed chief heat officers to coordinate policy. Some scholars argue that shade itself should be regarded as a public resource, unevenly distributed and politically contested, pointing to bus stops without shelters and the removal of trees in the name of security or visibility. Designing cities for increasingly extreme heat, they argue, requires attention not only to engineering but to questions of justice. Planting trees is often proposed as the simplest remedy, but trees take decades to mature and require water and maintenance. Effective adaptation, experts suggest, will demand long-term commitment rather than short-term gestures announced during each new heatwave.

### C2-12 | The Curious Case of the Placeholder Name
Every language needs words for things whose names are momentarily forgotten, unknown or deliberately left unspecified. English speakers reach for thingamajig, whatsit or doodah; Spanish speakers say chisme, and speakers of Mandarin may use the phrase nàge, meaning that one, which also functions as a hesitation filler. Linguists call these words placeholders, and although they have traditionally been dismissed as sloppy or uneducated speech, recent research suggests they perform sophisticated communicative work. Placeholders commonly emerge when speakers experience the tip-of-the-tongue state, the frustrating sensation of knowing a word while being temporarily unable to retrieve it. Psychologists have studied this phenomenon since the 1960s, when researchers at Harvard induced it by reading definitions of rare words such as sampan and asking participants to report anything they could recall. People in this state could frequently identify the first letter or the number of syllables of the missing word, indicating that retrieval had partially succeeded. Tip-of-the-tongue experiences become more frequent with age and are especially common for proper names. Placeholders allow conversation to continue fluently while signalling to the listener that collaboration is required, and listeners often respond by supplying the missing term. They also serve social purposes. A speaker may avoid naming something embarrassing or taboo, or may use vagueness to project casual informality, since precision can sound pedantic in relaxed conversation. In some languages placeholders are grammatically elaborate. In certain Australian Aboriginal languages and in Japanese, a placeholder can take the inflections of the word it replaces, so that a speaker might conjugate a substitute verb meaning roughly to do the thing in the appropriate tense before the intended verb has been retrieved. Such patterns demonstrate that speakers plan sentence structure ahead of selecting specific vocabulary, a finding consistent with models of speech production in which grammatical frames are assembled before content words are inserted. The study of placeholders thus illuminates the architecture of language processing itself. Historically, placeholder terms have also proved remarkably productive sources of vocabulary. The English word gadget, now entirely respectable, appears to have originated among nineteenth-century sailors as a term for any small mechanical device whose proper name escaped the speaker, and similar trajectories can be traced in numerous languages, reminding us that the boundaries between careless speech and standard vocabulary are rarely fixed. Placeholders, far from being signs of laziness, therefore reveal how speakers cooperate and how languages evolve. Researchers studying conversation now treat them as a valuable window into the mind.

### C2-13 | Mapping the Brain's Hidden Plumbing
For more than a century, anatomy textbooks taught that the brain lacked a lymphatic system, the network of vessels that elsewhere in the body drains excess fluid and removes waste products from tissues. This posed an awkward puzzle, because the brain is among the most metabolically active organs, generating substantial quantities of waste, and it is exquisitely sensitive to its chemical environment. How it cleared that waste was poorly understood. In 2012, a team led by the Danish neuroscientist Maiken Nedergaard, working at the University of Rochester in the United States, described a mechanism they named the glymphatic system, combining glial, a reference to the brain's supporting cells, with lymphatic. Using imaging techniques in mice, they observed that cerebrospinal fluid flows into brain tissue along channels surrounding arteries, mixes with the fluid between cells and then carries waste away along channels around veins. The movement depends partly on water channels in the cells called astrocytes. A year later, the same group reported a finding that attracted wide attention: the system was dramatically more active during sleep, when the space between brain cells expanded by roughly sixty percent, permitting faster flow. Among the substances removed more efficiently was beta-amyloid, a protein that accumulates in the brains of people with Alzheimer's disease. The result suggested a possible biological explanation for one of sleep's functions and for epidemiological links between poor sleep and dementia. In 2015, two independent groups also identified genuine lymphatic vessels lining the membranes that surround the brain, overturning the textbook doctrine and providing an exit route for fluid. Subsequent research has, however, generated considerable debate. Some laboratories have reported results inconsistent with the original model, arguing that fluid movement within brain tissue occurs mainly through diffusion rather than bulk flow, and a 2024 study in mice even found that clearance decreased during sleep, contradicting the earlier findings. Methodological differences, including the effects of anaesthesia and of injecting tracers, may account for some discrepancies, and measuring such processes in living human brains remains extremely difficult. Researchers agree that the brain possesses mechanisms for clearing waste and that these interact with sleep and vascular health in important ways, but the details are still contested. The episode illustrates how a compelling discovery can spread rapidly into popular understanding, often summarised as sleep washes the brain, well before the scientific community has reached consensus.

### C2-14 | The Logic of Insurance Against Catastrophe
Insurance rests on a simple principle: by pooling risks across many individuals, the unpredictable losses of a few can be covered by modest, predictable contributions from all. The principle works well when losses are independent of one another, as with car accidents or house fires, because the law of large numbers ensures that the total payout in a given year is relatively stable. It works far less well for catastrophes, in which a single event, such as a major earthquake or hurricane, can inflict losses on enormous numbers of policyholders simultaneously. After Hurricane Andrew struck Florida in 1992, causing damage far exceeding what insurers had anticipated, several companies became insolvent, and many others withdrew from the market. The industry responded in part by developing catastrophe models, computer simulations that combine scientific understanding of hazards with data on the location and vulnerability of buildings to estimate probable losses. It also created new financial instruments. Catastrophe bonds, first issued in the mid-1990s, allow insurers to transfer risk to investors in capital markets. Investors receive attractive interest payments, but if a specified disaster occurs, for example an earthquake exceeding a certain magnitude in a defined region, they lose some or all of their principal, which is used to pay claims. Because natural disasters are largely unrelated to stock market movements, such bonds appeal to investors seeking diversification. Some arrangements, known as parametric insurance, pay out automatically when a measurable trigger is reached, such as rainfall below a threshold, rather than after a lengthy assessment of actual damage. This speed has made them attractive for developing countries. The Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Facility, established in 2007 as the first multi-country pool of its kind, paid Haiti nearly eight million dollars within two weeks of the devastating 2010 earthquake. Parametric schemes suffer, however, from basis risk, the possibility that a policyholder suffers severe losses without the trigger being met, or vice versa. Climate change poses a deeper challenge to the entire model. As wildfires, floods and storms intensify, insurers in vulnerable regions, notably parts of California and Florida, have raised premiums sharply or stopped offering coverage altogether, leaving homeowners dependent on state-backed insurers of last resort. Economists warn that if insurance becomes unaffordable, property values may fall abruptly, with consequences for local tax revenues and mortgage lenders. Others argue that higher premiums transmit an essential price signal, discouraging construction in hazardous locations.

### C2-15 | Silence as a Musical Material
On 29 August 1952, in a concert hall in Woodstock, New York, the pianist David Tudor walked onto the stage, sat at the piano, closed its lid and started a stopwatch. Over the next four minutes and thirty-three seconds, divided into three movements marked by opening and closing the lid, he played nothing at all. The audience heard wind in the trees outside, then raindrops on the roof, and finally their own murmurs of bewilderment, some of which turned into indignation. The work, by the composer John Cage, became known as 4′33″ and remains one of the most debated compositions of the twentieth century. Cage maintained that the piece was not about silence but about listening. A visit to an anechoic chamber at Harvard University, a room designed to absorb all sound reflections, had convinced him that absolute silence was impossible; inside it, he reported hearing two sounds, one high and one low, which an engineer explained as his nervous system and his blood circulating. If silence could not exist, then the sounds of the environment could become the substance of music, and the composer's role was to frame them rather than to impose structure. Cage was influenced by Zen Buddhism, which he studied in New York, and by the white paintings of his friend Robert Rauschenberg, canvases whose blank surfaces registered shadows and dust from the room. Critics have accused the piece of being a hoax or a gesture of contempt for audiences, yet it has been performed by orchestras and rock musicians alike, and in 2010 a campaign in Britain sought to make a recording of it the most popular song at Christmas. Composers before Cage had, of course, used silence expressively. Rests have structured music for centuries, and the dramatic pauses in works by Beethoven or Haydn depend on the listener's expectation that sound will resume. What distinguished Cage was the elevation of silence, or rather unintended sound, from a frame for music to its entire content. His influence extended into minimalism, sound art and experimental performance, and contemporary research in acoustics and psychology has continued to explore how listeners perceive pauses, which can heighten attention, convey emotion and shape the perception of time. In an increasingly noisy world, some commentators see renewed relevance in Cage's invitation to attend to the sounds normally ignored. Recordings of the piece vary considerably, since each performance captures a different environment.
