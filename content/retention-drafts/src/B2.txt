### B2-01 | The Economics of Queues
Nobody enjoys waiting in line, yet queues are one of the most common experiences of modern life. Researchers who study waiting have found that how long we wait matters less than how the wait feels. In one famous case, passengers at an airport in Houston complained constantly about waiting for their luggage, even though the delay was only about eight minutes. The airport hired more staff, which reduced the wait, but complaints continued. Analysts then noticed that passengers walked for just one minute from the plane to the baggage area and spent the remaining seven minutes standing still. The airport moved the arrival gates further away, so that passengers had to walk for six minutes. The total time stayed almost the same, but complaints nearly disappeared. Psychologists explain that unoccupied time feels longer than occupied time, and uncertain waits feel longer than known ones. This is why many lifts have mirrors beside them, and why theme parks display estimated waiting times, which are often deliberately slightly longer than the real ones so that visitors feel pleased when they reach the front early. Fairness also plays a role. People become much angrier when someone pushes in than when the line is simply slow, and a single queue feeding several counters is generally preferred to separate lines, even when it looks longer, because nobody is stuck behind a slow customer while the next line moves. Businesses that understand these principles can improve customer satisfaction without actually making anyone wait less, although critics argue that this is a form of manipulation.

### B2-02 | The Library of Seeds
On a remote Norwegian island halfway between the mainland and the North Pole, a tunnel leads more than one hundred metres into a frozen mountain. At the end are three chambers containing over a million packets of seeds from almost every country in the world. The Svalbard Global Seed Vault, opened in 2008, is designed as a backup for the thousands of smaller seed banks that store crop varieties around the globe. If a national collection is destroyed by war, natural disaster or simple mismanagement, copies can be retrieved from Svalbard. The location was chosen carefully. The permafrost keeps the seeds cold even if the electricity fails, the area is geologically stable, and the vault sits high enough above sea level to stay dry even if all the polar ice melts. Countries that deposit seeds keep ownership of them; only they can ask for them to be returned. The first withdrawal happened in 2015, when researchers from Syria's civil war-damaged seed bank in Aleppo requested samples of wheat, barley and lentils adapted to dry conditions. They replanted them in Lebanon and Morocco and later sent new seeds back to the vault. Not everything has gone to plan. In 2017, unusually warm temperatures caused meltwater to flood the entrance tunnel, although none of the seeds were damaged. The incident led to expensive improvements and became a symbol of the very climate problems the vault was built to protect against. Visitors are not allowed inside, but the striking entrance, lit by an art installation, has become one of the most photographed buildings in the Arctic.

### B2-03 | Why Some Cities Are Louder at Night
Most people think of noise pollution as a minor irritation, but health researchers increasingly treat it as a serious public health issue. The World Health Organization estimates that long-term exposure to traffic noise contributes to thousands of deaths from heart disease in Europe every year. The main danger is not loud, sudden sounds but steady background noise at night. Even when people do not wake up, their bodies react: heart rate rises and stress hormones are released, which over years can raise blood pressure. Noise does not affect everyone equally. In many cities, cheaper housing is located near motorways, railways and airports, so poorer residents tend to sleep with more noise. Some cities are responding with creative solutions. Paris has tested cameras that use microphones to identify vehicles breaking noise limits, similar to speed cameras, and fine their owners automatically. Several cities in the Netherlands have installed quieter road surfaces that absorb sound from tyres, which is the main source of traffic noise at speeds above about thirty kilometres an hour. Others are planting thick hedges and building walls covered with plants along busy roads. Architects, meanwhile, design buildings with bedrooms on the quiet side, away from the street. Experts note that electric cars help less than many people assume, because at higher speeds tyre noise is far louder than the engine. Reducing speed limits, they argue, may be the cheapest and most effective step. In the meantime, many residents simply buy earplugs or heavy curtains, which help a little but do not solve the underlying problem.

### B2-04 | The Accidental Inventor
In 1945, an American engineer named Percy Spencer was working on radar equipment for the military when he noticed that a chocolate bar in his pocket had melted. Many people might have ignored this, but Spencer was curious. He placed popcorn kernels near the machine, and they began to pop. The next day he brought an egg, which exploded in a colleague's face. Spencer realised that the microwaves produced by the radar's magnetron were heating food from the inside. His company, Raytheon, patented the idea, and in 1947 it sold the first commercial microwave oven. It was nearly two metres tall, weighed about 340 kilograms and cost thousands of dollars, so it was used only in restaurants, ships and railway carriages. It took about twenty more years before smaller, affordable models appeared in ordinary kitchens. Spencer's story is often told as an example of a lucky accident, but historians point out that luck was only part of it. Spencer had left school at twelve and taught himself engineering, and he was known for his practical skill and persistence. Other workers had experienced similar effects without investigating them. As the scientist Louis Pasteur said a century earlier, chance favours the prepared mind. Spencer received a one-time payment of two dollars for the patent, which was the standard amount his company paid employees for inventions, although he later became a senior vice president of the firm. Today, microwave ovens are found in more than ninety percent of households in some countries, a success Spencer could hardly have imagined.

### B2-05 | Rewilding the Highlands
For centuries, large parts of the Scottish Highlands have been bare hillsides covered with grass and heather. Many visitors find these landscapes beautiful and assume they are natural, but ecologists point out that the area was once covered by a vast forest of pine, birch and oak. The trees disappeared gradually as a result of farming, timber cutting and, especially, grazing by sheep and deer. Today, red deer have no natural predators in Scotland, because wolves were hunted to extinction in the eighteenth century, and their population has grown so large that young trees rarely survive. Several projects are now trying to restore the forest, an approach known as rewilding. On one estate, owners have reduced deer numbers sharply by culling and built fences around new woodland. Within a decade, trees that had been eaten as soon as they appeared started growing, and birds, insects and small mammals returned. Some campaigners want to go further and reintroduce lynx or even wolves to control deer naturally. This idea is highly controversial. Farmers fear for their sheep, and some rural communities feel that wealthy landowners and city-based organisations are deciding the future of land they do not live on. Supporters respond that rewilding can create jobs in tourism and conservation. In 2009, beavers were officially released in Scotland for the first time in four hundred years, and their dams have already improved wetland habitats. The debate is likely to continue for many years, because it raises difficult questions about who the countryside is really for and what natural should mean.

### B2-06 | The Science of Procrastination
Almost everyone delays tasks they know they should do, but psychologists now believe that procrastination is not mainly a problem of laziness or poor time management. Instead, it is a problem of managing emotions. When a task makes us feel anxious, bored or insecure, avoiding it provides immediate relief, and the brain rewards that relief. The cost, however, appears later, in the form of stress and lower-quality work. Researchers at a Canadian university found that students who forgave themselves for procrastinating before one exam were less likely to procrastinate before the next. This surprised many people, who expected that being strict with oneself would work better. In fact, harsh self-criticism seems to increase negative emotions, which makes avoidance more attractive. Another factor is the way we think about our future selves. Brain scans suggest that when people imagine themselves in ten years, their brain activity resembles the pattern seen when they think about a stranger. As a result, we feel less concerned about the consequences of our decisions for that future person. Practical strategies include breaking a large task into a very small first step, such as writing a single sentence, and removing distractions such as phones from the room. Some people use the so-called two-minute rule: if something takes less than two minutes, do it immediately. Experts warn that occasional delay is normal, but chronic procrastination has been linked to poorer health. Understanding the emotional side of the habit, researchers say, is the first step towards changing it, and it is never too late to begin.

### B2-07 | The Hidden Cost of Fast Fashion
Over the last twenty years, the amount of clothing produced worldwide has roughly doubled, while the average number of times each item is worn before being thrown away has fallen sharply. This trend, known as fast fashion, is driven by companies that release new designs every few weeks at very low prices. The environmental cost is considerable. Producing a single cotton T-shirt can require around 2,700 litres of water, mostly to grow the cotton. Synthetic fabrics such as polyester are made from oil and release tiny plastic fibres when washed, which end up in rivers and oceans. Dyeing and finishing fabric is also a major source of water pollution in producing countries. Many people believe that donating old clothes solves the problem, but charities receive far more than they can sell. A large proportion is exported to countries such as Ghana, where the Kantamanto market in Accra receives millions of second-hand garments every week. Traders there say that much of it is of such poor quality that it cannot be sold, and it ends up in landfill or on beaches. Some brands now offer repair services or take-back schemes, and the European Union is planning rules that will make producers responsible for the waste their products create. Critics argue, however, that the most effective solution is simply to buy fewer clothes and wear them for longer, a message that is difficult for an industry built on constant novelty. Some young consumers are already doing this, buying second-hand clothes online or swapping items with friends instead of shopping for new ones.

### B2-08 | The Lost City in the Jungle
For centuries, stories circulated in Honduras about a white city hidden in the rainforest of the Mosquitia region, a place with dense vegetation, venomous snakes and almost no roads. Explorers searched for it many times without success. In 2012, a team of filmmakers and scientists took a different approach. They flew over the forest in a small plane equipped with lidar, a technology that sends millions of laser pulses towards the ground and measures how long they take to return. Some of the pulses pass through gaps in the leaves, which allows a computer to create a detailed map of the land beneath the trees. The images revealed the outlines of plazas, earthworks and a pyramid-like structure, suggesting a large settlement. When archaeologists reached the site on foot in 2015, they found a remarkable collection of stone sculptures, including figures that appeared to be half human and half jaguar. The city was probably built between 1000 and 1500 and was not connected to the Maya, as many had assumed, but to a separate, little-known culture. Shortly after the expedition, several team members fell ill with a parasitic disease spread by sand flies, a reminder of the dangers of the environment. The government has since sent soldiers to protect the area from looters and illegal logging. Lidar has also transformed research elsewhere, revealing thousands of hidden Maya structures in Guatemala. Researchers believe that many more ancient settlements are still waiting to be discovered beneath the forests of Central America and elsewhere.

### B2-09 | Can Plants Hear?
It may sound like science fiction, but a growing number of experiments suggest that plants respond to sound. In one study, researchers in the United States recorded the vibrations made by caterpillars chewing leaves and played them to a small plant called Arabidopsis. Plants that had heard the chewing produced more defensive chemicals when they were later attacked by real caterpillars than plants that had been kept in silence. Importantly, the plants did not react in the same way to recordings of wind or insect songs with similar frequencies. Another team in Israel found that the flowers of the beach evening primrose produced sweeter nectar within three minutes of being exposed to the sound of a flying bee. Flowers covered by a glass jar, which blocked the vibrations, showed no change. The researchers suggested that the bowl shape of the flower may work like an ear, collecting and amplifying the sound. Scientists have also recorded plants under stress making faint clicking noises in the ultrasonic range, which humans cannot hear. Tomato plants that had not been watered for several days produced dozens of these clicks every hour. Some researchers speculate that insects or other animals might use these sounds to detect weakened plants. Many botanists remain cautious. Plants have no nervous system, and the mechanisms are not yet understood. Nonetheless, the findings challenge the traditional idea that plants are passive organisms unaware of their surroundings. Further experiments, scientists agree, will be needed before anyone can say with confidence what plants are really able to perceive.

### B2-10 | The Return of the Bookshop
Not long ago, independent bookshops seemed to be heading for extinction. Online retailers offered huge discounts and fast delivery, e-readers were growing quickly, and large chains were closing branches. In the United Kingdom, the number of independent bookshops fell for more than twenty years, reaching a low point of fewer than nine hundred in 2016. Since then, however, the figure has risen steadily. Analysts give several reasons for this recovery. Sales of e-books stopped growing after the early 2010s, as many readers discovered that they still preferred printed books, especially for gifts and children's books. At the same time, successful shops reinvented themselves as community spaces rather than simple places to buy products. They host author talks, reading groups and children's story times, and many have added cafés. Staff recommendations, often handwritten on cards, give customers a sense of discovery that algorithms rarely provide. Social media has also helped, as readers share photos of attractive shop interiors and new titles. During the pandemic, many shops survived by offering local delivery, sometimes by bicycle, and customers made a deliberate effort to support them. The business remains difficult. Profit margins are small, rents in town centres are high, and online discounts have not disappeared. Some owners combine their shop with other activities, such as selling stationery or running courses, to make ends meet. Still, the shops' revival shows that convenience is not the only thing customers value. Many owners say that their customers come not just for books but for conversation, advice and the pleasure of browsing without hurry.

### B2-11 | The Invisible Workforce of Bees
When people think of bees, they usually imagine honeybees living in hives and making honey. Yet there are around twenty thousand species of bee worldwide, and most of them live alone, without a queen or a hive. These solitary bees nest in holes in the ground, hollow stems or gaps in walls, and many are excellent pollinators. Some species are far more efficient than honeybees at pollinating particular crops. The red mason bee, for example, carries pollen loosely on the hairs of its abdomen rather than packing it neatly, so more grains fall onto each flower it visits. Studies in orchards have found that a few hundred mason bees can pollinate as many apple trees as thousands of honeybees. Bumblebees, meanwhile, use a technique called buzz pollination: they grip a flower and vibrate their flight muscles, shaking pollen loose. Tomatoes and blueberries are pollinated much more effectively in this way. Wild bees are in serious decline in many regions because of habitat loss, pesticides and disease. Some conservationists worry that keeping more honeybee hives, often promoted as a way to save bees, can make the problem worse in cities, because large numbers of honeybees compete with wild species for limited flowers. They argue that planting a variety of native flowers, leaving patches of bare soil and reducing pesticide use would help wild bees more. Even a small garden with untidy corners can provide valuable habitat. Gardeners who leave dandelions and clover in their lawns, rather than cutting them immediately, provide some of the earliest food of the year.

### B2-12 | A Village That Banned Cars
Pontevedra, a small city in north-western Spain, used to suffer from the same problems as many European towns: traffic jams, pollution and a historic centre that people drove through but rarely stayed in. In 1999, a newly elected mayor, Miguel Anxo Fernández Lores, decided to make radical changes. Within a short time, his administration banned private cars from the medieval centre, except for deliveries, emergency vehicles and residents going to their garages. Street parking was replaced by wide pavements, and large free car parks were built on the edge of the centre. Elsewhere, the speed limit was reduced to thirty kilometres an hour, and traffic lights were removed and replaced with raised pedestrian crossings. The decision was very unpopular at first, and local businesses predicted disaster. The results, however, have been striking. Carbon dioxide emissions from traffic fell by about two thirds, and no one has died in a traffic accident in the centre for many years. Children walk to school on their own along marked routes, and the centre's population grew as families moved in, while many similar towns lost residents. Shop owners who once protested now generally support the scheme, because pedestrians visit more often. Critics note that Pontevedra is small and relatively flat, so its model may be difficult to copy in larger cities. Nonetheless, urban planners from around the world now visit to study how the city reclaimed its streets for people. The mayor has since been re-elected several times, which suggests that most residents are happy with the changes made to their city.

### B2-13 | Memory Palaces
Competitive memory athletes can memorise the order of a shuffled deck of cards in less than twenty seconds or recall hundreds of random numbers after a few minutes of study. Surprisingly, brain scans show that most of them do not have unusually large brains or exceptional natural ability. Instead, they rely on techniques, the most famous of which is the method of loci, also known as the memory palace. The technique dates back to ancient Greece. According to legend, the poet Simonides was the only survivor when a banqueting hall collapsed, and he was able to identify the victims by remembering where each guest had been sitting. To use the method, a person imagines a familiar place, such as their home, and mentally places vivid images representing the information to be remembered at specific locations along a route. Later, they walk through the place in their mind and collect the images. The method works because human memory is particularly good at remembering spaces and unusual pictures. In a study published in 2017, researchers trained ordinary volunteers in the technique for forty days, about thirty minutes a day. Their ability to remember lists of words more than doubled, and four months later they still performed much better than a control group. Their patterns of brain connectivity had also become more similar to those of memory champions. Teachers have begun using adapted versions of the method to help students learn vocabulary and historical facts.

### B2-14 | The Sound of Silence in Space
Science fiction films often show spaceships exploding with tremendous noise, but in reality space is almost completely silent. Sound is a vibration that travels through a medium such as air or water, and space is a near-perfect vacuum, with too few particles to carry these vibrations. Astronauts on a spacewalk can talk to each other only by radio, which uses electromagnetic waves rather than sound. Inside the International Space Station, however, the situation is very different. The air needed for breathing allows sound to travel normally, and the station is surprisingly noisy because of fans and pumps that run constantly to circulate air and remove carbon dioxide. Without moving air, the carbon dioxide breathed out by a sleeping astronaut would collect around their head. Early in the station's history, noise levels were high enough to cause concern about hearing damage, and astronauts were given earplugs and regular hearing tests. Engineers have since installed quieter equipment and sound-absorbing materials. Interestingly, space is not entirely silent in a scientific sense. Clouds of gas and plasma around planets and stars can carry very low-frequency pressure waves. NASA has converted data from such waves around a black hole in the Perseus galaxy cluster into audible sound by raising the pitch dozens of octaves, producing an eerie, deep hum. These recordings are scientific translations rather than sounds anyone could hear directly, but they help the public understand phenomena that are otherwise invisible. For now, astronauts say that the constant hum of machinery quickly becomes background noise, and some even miss it after returning to Earth.

### B2-15 | The Power of a Nap
In many cultures, an afternoon nap is a normal part of the day, while in others it is seen as a sign of laziness. Scientific research increasingly suggests that a short nap can bring real benefits. A well-known NASA study of pilots on long flights found that a planned nap of about twenty-six minutes improved their alertness by more than fifty percent and their performance by about a third. The length of a nap is important. Sleep happens in cycles of roughly ninety minutes, and during the deeper stages the brain becomes harder to wake. A nap of ten to twenty minutes usually keeps a person in light sleep, so they wake up feeling refreshed. A longer nap of around forty-five minutes may lead to sleep inertia, a feeling of grogginess and confusion that can last half an hour. A full ninety-minute nap can support memory and creativity, because it includes a complete cycle, but it may make it harder to fall asleep at night. Some people combine a cup of coffee with a short nap, a so-called coffee nap. Caffeine takes about twenty minutes to have an effect, so drinking it just before lying down means it starts working as the person wakes. Researchers caution that frequent long daytime naps in older adults have been associated with health problems, although it is unclear whether the naps cause these problems or are a sign of them. For most healthy adults, though, a short nap in the early afternoon appears to be a safe and effective way to recover energy.
